# When the Money Runs Short: Financial Strain and Student Dropout

**Team 4 · Ten-minute presentation.** Start on the Everyone Walks home page with all filters reset. Times include the chart changes and pauses. Suggested handoffs follow the team's content, dashboard and presentation roles.

### 0:00–1:00 · Merachew · The question

*[Show the cover. Choose “Follow the financial story.”]*

Imagine an advisor has time for one more call today. The billing office can see that a student has fallen behind. Is that a useful moment to reach out? And if the advisor only calls students on that list, who will they miss?

Those two questions shaped our project. We studied 4,424 students at one Portuguese higher education institution. We looked at three things a campus might already record: whether tuition was current, whether a student had outstanding debt, and whether they held a scholarship. We wanted to know how those circumstances lined up with leaving college, and what a financial aid or advising team could reasonably do with that information.

### 1:00–2:00 · Merachew · The outcomes donut

*[Point to each outcome. Pause on “Still enrolled.”]*

Each record represents one student. By the end of the normal program length, 2,209 had graduated, 794 were still enrolled, and 1,421 had dropped out. That last group is roughly one in three. We keep students who were still enrolled in the denominator. They were continuing their studies when the outcome was recorded, and their eventual result is unknown.

Keep two questions in mind as we move from this donut to the financial charts. First, within a group of students, how common was dropout? Second, among everyone who dropped out, how many belonged to that group? The first question tells us where a check-in looks especially relevant. The second tells us how far that check-in could reach. Cartez will show why those answers can look quite different.

### 2:00–3:35 · Cartez · Tuition and the two denominators

*[Choose “Compare financial outcomes.” Point first to the rate, then to the share of departures.]*

Of the 528 students whose tuition was behind, 457 dropped out. That is 86.6%. Among students whose tuition was current, the dropout rate was 24.7%. If a payment falls behind, that difference gives financial aid a practical reason to offer a conversation about costs.

Now look at the size of the current-tuition group. It included 3,896 students. Even with a much lower dropout rate, 964 of them left. Those 964 account for about 68% of all departures in the study. An outreach rule based on late tuition alone would have included fewer than a third of the students who eventually left.

There is also a timing question. These records tell us a student's payment status, but they do not tell us when the institution recorded it. Before launching a pilot, the billing team needs to establish when an advisor could actually see a missed payment and whether there is still time to offer useful help.

### 3:35–4:35 · Cartez · Outstanding debt

*[Move to the debtor chart. Show the student counts under the group labels.]*

Outstanding debt gives us a second view of financial strain. Of 503 students with recorded debt, 312 dropped out, or 62.0%. Among students without recorded debt, 28.3% dropped out.

Why keep this chart alongside tuition? A student can be current on tuition and still have an outstanding balance. Also, the same student can appear in both financial comparisons. Adding the two charts together would count some students twice. For a campus team, the practical implication is to check what the balance represents and coordinate one invitation. A student should be able to explain the situation once and get connected with the office that can help.

### 4:35–6:05 · Cartez · Scholarships and staying in college

*[Show the stacked scholarship bars. Trace one whole row before comparing the dropout and graduate segments.]*

Each bar represents all the students in one scholarship group. Among the 1,099 scholarship holders, 134 dropped out. That is 12.2%, compared with 38.7% among students without a scholarship. The difference is about 26.5 percentage points.

The graduation segments add something the dropout figures alone cannot tell us. Among scholarship holders, 835 graduated, or 76.0%. Among students without a scholarship, 41.3% graduated. The remaining segment in each row represents students still enrolled. Keeping that segment visible helps us distinguish continuing a program from completing it.

Look at the tuition and scholarship chart together. Among students behind on tuition without a scholarship, 89.0% dropped out. Among students current on tuition with a scholarship, 10.1% dropped out. Both payment status and award status differ between those groups. Eligibility rules, program mix, or prior achievement could also matter. We cannot use this gap to predict the effect of awarding a scholarship. We can use it to ask who qualifies, who applies, and where eligible students lose access to support. Carson will connect that question to an outreach plan.

### 6:05–7:25 · Carson · An advising checkpoint

*[Choose “Explore first-semester progress.” Show the two outreach rules.]*

We saw earlier that most students who left had current tuition. Once first-term results arrive, advising has another opportunity to offer support. Among students who passed under half of their enrolled units, 84.8% dropped out. Among those who passed at least half, 18.4% dropped out. These comparisons exclude the 180 students who enrolled in no first-term units because we cannot calculate a pass rate for them.

In this historical cohort, combining the late-tuition and low-pass rules would have identified 1,115 students, including 919 of the 1,421 who dropped out. Compared with tuition outreach alone, that adds 587 invitations and includes 462 additional students who later left. That is a staffing decision a team can discuss. It does not tell us how many departures a check-in would prevent.

### 7:25–8:30 · Carson · A brief live decision

*[Open Outreach planner with all cohort filters reset. Select “Missed tuition payment.” Expand “Can our team cover these invitations?” and enter 500 appointments. Pause, then select “Add a first-semester advising check-in.”]*

Suppose the office has capacity for 500 appointments. That is an example budget for this demonstration. The tuition rule alone gives us 528 invitations, already 28 beyond that capacity if we reserve a place for everyone. Adding first-term results brings the difference to 615 beyond capacity.

The value of this tool is being able to see the workload before making a promise to students. A team could stagger invitations, add advising hours, or reserve time for students who ask for help directly. The dashboard lets us see the trade-off; the campus still has to decide how to deliver the service.

### 8:30–10:00 · Carson · Three actions and a pilot

*[Open Recommendations. Leave this screen visible for the close.]*

Our first recommendation is to treat a missed payment as a prompt for a supportive check-in. Once billing confirms the record is timely, financial aid could contact the student within weeks, explain why they are reaching out, and ask whether cost is getting in the way of continuing.

Second, make the offer concrete. Explain available payment plans and emergency aid, help the student identify the next step, and arrange an advising introduction with permission. Give someone responsibility for following up so the student knows whom to contact.

Third, review scholarship access. Follow applications from eligibility through award. If eligible students start an application and stop, ask where they encountered difficulty. That gives the office a specific process to improve and a way to assess whether the change helped.

For a pilot, track invitations, completed conversations, aid received, and registration the following term. Compare outcomes with a similar group receiving usual support. Our evidence comes from one historical institution, so a local pilot is how we would learn whether the approach works here.

If you take one thing away, let it be the contrast in the tuition chart. Falling behind was associated with a high dropout rate, yet most students who left were current on tuition. Start the financial conversation early, and give students more than one way to reach support.
